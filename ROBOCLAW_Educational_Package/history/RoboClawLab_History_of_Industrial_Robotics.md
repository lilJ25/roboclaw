# RoboClawLab Mini-Book  
## A Short History of Industrial Robotics  
(Unimate to the Present – Industrial Arms Only)

This concise timeline covers only industrial robotics (programmable arms and related factory systems). Humanoid and service robots are excluded. Sources include IEEE Spectrum, the International Federation of Robotics (IFR), manufacturer historical records (KUKA, ABB/ASEA, Unimation), and peer-reviewed historical reviews.

---

### 1950s – The Beginning
- **1954** – George Devol files the U.S. patent for a “Programmed Article Transfer” device – the conceptual foundation of the programmable industrial robot.¹
- **1956** – Devol and Joseph Engelberger found Unimation, the world’s first industrial robotics company.¹ ²
- **1959–1961** – The first Unimate robot is built and installed. In 1961 it begins work at a General Motors plant in Trenton, New Jersey, extracting hot die-cast parts – the first commercial industrial robot application.¹ ³

### 1960s – First Commercial Deployments
- Hydraulic Unimate robots expand in U.S. automotive plants (spot welding, machine tending).³
- **1967–1969** – Technology licensed to Kawasaki in Japan; the first Japanese-built Unimate appears. Europe also begins installations.² ⁴
- **1969** – Victor Scheinman develops the Stanford Arm – the first electrically powered, computer-controlled robotic arm, strongly influencing later designs.⁵

### 1970s – Electric Drive and Microprocessors Arrive
- **1973** – KUKA introduces the FAMULUS, the world’s first industrial robot with six electromechanically driven axes (electric motors instead of hydraulics).⁶ ⁷
- **1974** – ASEA (later part of ABB) launches the IRB 6 – the world’s first fully electric, microprocessor-controlled industrial robot (approximately 6 kg payload). First customer installations follow shortly afterward.⁸ ⁹
- **1975** – ASEA introduces the IRB 60 for higher payloads (spot welding applications).⁸
- Cincinnati Milacron and others bring microcomputer-controlled robots to market.²
- Unimation’s PUMA (Programmable Universal Machine for Assembly) appears later in the decade and becomes a standard for precision assembly.² ⁵

### 1980s – Maturity and Specialization
- Electric robots largely replace hydraulic designs for most applications.²
- ASEA/ABB introduce dedicated models for spot welding (IRB 90), painting, and higher-speed assembly.⁸ ⁹
- Controllers improve; AC servo motors become standard.⁸
- Industrial robot installations grow rapidly, especially in automotive and electronics.²

### 1990s – Parallel Robots and Better Controllers
- **1992** – ABB introduces the S4 controller with major improvements in path accuracy and user interface.⁹
- Parallel (Delta-style) kinematics mature for high-speed pick-and-place.²
- **1998** – ABB launches the FlexPicker (IRB 340), a commercial high-speed Delta robot for packaging.⁹
- KUKA and others expand PC-based control and open architecture.⁶

### 2000s–2010s – Compact High-Performance Arms
- Small, fast 6-axis robots become common for electronics, packaging, and laboratory work.
- **2009** – ABB launches the IRB 120 (25 kg robot mass, 3 kg payload, 580 mm reach) – one of the smallest multipurpose industrial robots of its time.¹⁰
- KUKA launches and evolves the Agilus series of compact high-speed robots (including the KR 10 R1100-2 generation).⁶ ¹¹
- Collaborative features, better safety systems, and higher repeatability become standard across the industry.
- Global industrial robot stock grows into the millions (IFR statistics).²

### 2020s – Current Landscape
Modern industrial robots emphasize:
- Compact footprints and high cycle rates (the class represented by the KUKA KR 10 R1100-2 and ABB IRB 120)
- Easy integration with vision, force sensing, and digital twins
- Energy efficiency and simplified programming
- Continued dominance in automotive, electronics, food, packaging, and logistics

Today more than three million industrial robots are estimated to be operating worldwide.²

---

**Notes for students**  
Industrial robotics progressed from hydraulic, open-loop machines performing single hazardous tasks to precise, electrically driven, multi-axis systems capable of complex paths at high speed. The two robots used in RoboClaw labs sit at the modern end of this continuum: small, fast, highly repeatable arms designed for the kinds of tasks that once required larger, slower machines or human labor in constrained spaces.
